Campaign & Character Creation

Campaign Setup

“Atropos roughly tore the thread of flourishing life.”
— Statius

This phase is almost entirely Storyteller work. Player input is limited to ranking concrete campaign seeds. Character creation comes later.

If you are a player reading ahead: you only need the constraints and the seeds you are asked to rank. Everything else on this page is for the person preparing the table.

Interest Signal

Section titled “Interest Signal”

Players receive only a ranked list of campaign seeds and the system constraints. Nothing else written. If someone asks about a specific name, answer in two spoken sentences at most.

Constraints (state once)

No magic. Pre-industrial tools and knowledge only. Ordinary human beings.
The community is the real protagonist. If it is destroyed, the campaign ends.
Death is permanent.

What players do

Rank your top three seeds. Ask at most two spoken sentences about any name. Wait for the Storyteller to frame the community — then create characters.

The seeds below are examples only. Replace them freely. Players rank their top three.

A good seed uses a real or invented place name (not a vague label like “river country”), names a community, a real pressure (war, law, plague, tax, famine, migration), and a decision that cannot wait. It is not a list of horrors. Grim reality is the weather of the age — not the product you are selling.

Campaign seeds

Section titled “Campaign seeds”

Browse by setting type

Historical: locked place and year. Semi-historical: one clear break from a real situation. Invented: same weight as history, without dressing up misery for effect.

The Debatable Land, 1320s

  • Who — Related Border households on ground claimed by both English and Scottish lords.
  • Pressure — Both sides demand service and payment. The old middle-ground bargains are dead. Harvest was thin; winter is close.
  • Choose — Whom to serve, or refuse, and still be a people in spring.

Iceland, late Commonwealth — after the burnings

  • Who — Two neighbouring chieftains’ followings sharing a valley of hay and driftwood rights.
  • Pressure — Last winter halls burned and men died. Killers still in the district; blood-price unpaid. Althing in weeks. Hay must come in first.
  • Choose — Hold the hay, settle the dead without finishing the kinship, or lose the following to stronger goðar before court.

The Karadağ villages, after Manzikert (c. 1080s→)

  • Basis — Byzantine collapse in central Anatolia after Manzikert.
  • Break — Free villages on the Karadağ road never absorbed into a stable beylik or restored theme.
  • Pressure — A Turkic lord means permanent garrisons. A coastal Byzantine agent offers arms for a rising and a “relief” that may never come. Grain short; young men leaving as hirelings.
  • Choose — Take the garrison, rise for relief that may not arrive, or empty the hills and lose the name on the road to the coast.

Straumfjǫrðr held (c. 1020→)

  • Basis — Norse landings in Vinland (saga place-name).
  • Break — The settlement at Straumfjǫrðr did not fail; three generations of thin coastal freeholders remain.
  • Pressure — Iron scarce; Greenland rare; hard winters; uneasy neighbours after raids both ways. A Christian magnate’s longship arrives to claim the fort as a mission and reorganise freeholding under their law.
  • Choose — Resist without iron, accept and lose free status, or settle with the forest peoples and become strangers to Greenland.

Harrowden Vale, after the levy

  • Who — Freeholders of Harrowden Vale who fought for the losing side.
  • Pressure — Victors hold Longford and Millspan. Arms to be surrendered, grain levy due before seed-in, hostages from leading houses. Winter stores spent in the campaign.
  • Choose — Disarm under the levy, keep arms and face the next column, or buy months with hostages and live with the cost inside your own halls.

Ordmouth under siege, fifth month

  • Who — Citizen houses of Ordmouth on the Greywater; players tied to a few of them.
  • Pressure — Siege since harvest. Foreign unpaid garrison. Granaries still in citizen hands. Peace terms: open a gate, new garrison, named leaders for trial. Disease in the poorer quarters. Houses do not agree.
  • Choose — Open the gate, hold for the thaw, or force a third bargain before hunger and sickness decide.

The Vardmark at Kelarn’s Bend

  • Who — The Vardmark: a war-following from inhospitable steppe and high country. Conquerors, not farmers.
  • Pressure — They helped sack Kelarn and hold the ford with spears. Looted grain and cattle will not last the year. Survivors in marshes and half-empty towns. Rival war-bands on the next bend. Winter coming for people who know the raid better than the plough.
  • Choose — Rule the conquered as masters, live only by the raid until someone stronger takes the Bend, or break trying to become what you destroyed.

Worldbuilding Practices

Section titled “Worldbuilding Practices”

Do these in order. Prep situations — unstable circumstances, people with goals, places under pressure — not a fixed sequence of scenes. The story is what happens when the table acts. You may know more than you show; you do not open with a lore dump.

The worked thread below follows The Vardmark at Kelarn’s Bend. Change the seed; keep the method.

Worldbuilding walkthrough

Nine steps. Enough material for weight and choice. Not a novel the players must recite.

Lock the spectrum — tightly historical, semi-historical, or fully invented — and keep it for the whole campaign.

ExampleFully invented: the Vardmark occupy Kelarn’s Bend after they helped sack Kelarn — conquerors on ground they do not know how to farm. Made-up names are fine; hunger, weather, and armed neighbours still work as in history.
Best practiceWrite the spectrum on the first line of your notes. Historical: real seasons, law, and armies constrain you — use that. Semi-historical: change one hinge, then honour the consequences. Invented: same gravity of hunger, weather, and power. Do not invent away the cost of living, and do not invent cruelty for spectacle.

How does this people stay alive from season to season? Work, food, shelter, who settles quarrels. Where characters “should” be when they are not on a job.

ExampleMost of the Vardmark still live as a war-following: wagons, hide tents, and half-raised halls at Kelarn’s Bend. They eat looted grain, drive stolen cattle, hunt the reed-edge, and send out forage parties. A few smiths keep gear fit for the next fight. Children mind stock from the march. They do not plough; the people who knew these fields are dead, fled, or held as captives. There is little leisure — only the next take, the next watch, the next argument over who owns what was stolen.
Best practiceMaterial life is the foundation of the world. Know the hard season and who is free in it. If play never touches how people eat and work, the place feels hollow. When player plans pull people off the watch or the forage, the community should notice — that friction is story. If you cannot say how they get through late winter, fix that before you invent another faction. Do not give conquerors the skills of the people they just destroyed unless play earns that change.

Name the concrete things whose failure ends this community as a community. Stores, rights, places, tools, bonds — countable and at risk.

ExampleThe remaining looted grain and cattle at the Bend; the ford at Kelarn’s Bend; the wagons and mounts that still let them move as a war-band; control of the captives who know the fields and fords; the claim on the burned capital’s remaining stores; the absence of any living king of Kelarn who could lawfully regrant or redeem the land.
Best practiceFive to eight items. Each should be able to drive a session if threatened, stolen, bargained, or defended. Prefer things that force choices between households (who eats the last sack of grain? who holds the ford at night? who owns a captive who can work the soil?). An empty throne can be one of these — not as backstory to recite, but as a gap everyone feels when a decision needs a name on it.

One present-tense strain sentence per item. What is wrong this season. Who is pressing. What cannot wait.

ExampleLooted grain will not feed the following through late winter. The Red Banner has camped a day’s ride upriver and is stripping the same countryside. Marsh survivors will only trade fish if the Vardmark return captives from the sack. The ford is still contested after dark. Without a king of Kelarn, no seal can make the occupation “lawful” — every bargain is personal and may be broken by the next stronger hand.
Best practiceStrain is a situation the players can walk into, not a monologue. Prefer deadlines, faces, and pressure over adjectives. If nobody acts before the next moon, the community loses options — food, ground, or unity. Prep what would happen if the table did nothing. If the sentence could sit on any map, make it local to this place and season — use real or invented names, not vague labels.

Name only the people you need to make those strains playable. A few individuals and houses with wants and fears — enough to bind scenes together, not a cast list for a novel.

ExampleWar-leader Torven, who means to keep the following as a pure war-band and will not stay still. House Asren, which holds most of the remaining loot and the best captives, and wants them worked like property, not freed or traded cheap. Marsh elder Sera of the Kelarn remnant, willing to deal only if captives come home. Rival captain Jorun of the Red Banner, who wants the ford for himself. The absent king of Kelarn is only a fact: no seal, no court, every promise weaker for it.
Best practicePrep people with leverage and goals, not ranks or titles to recite. For each: what they want this season, what they will not forgive, what they do if left alone. Name places and people when it ties the story together — not to dump a gazetteer in session one. Leave room for the table to meet them in play and form their own judgments.

Do not pre-solve the social map for the players. Who is trusted, who leads, who is owed — that is best discovered in play through action, rumour, and consequence. You may hold private notes. You do not open with a lecture on who sits where.

ExampleYou know Torven expects obedience and House Asren expects a voice over loot and captives. You do not tell the table the order of standing. They learn it when someone gives an order and is obeyed — or is not — and when a bargain holds or breaks.
Best practiceConnections, standing, and hierarchy emerge from fiction. Naming every rung of power up front is a lore dump: it kills immersion and steals discovery. Hold what you need for consistency; reveal through scenes. If a bookkeeping diagram of standing exists for your own use, keep it off the table until play has earned the right to fill it. The point of this step is blank space the players walk into.

Three or four visible marks of recent history — things anyone can point at without a speech.

ExampleBlackened orchard rows on the approach to ruined Kelarn. Wagon-ruts and picket lines where the Vardmark war-camp has sat all winter. The ford at Kelarn’s Bend marked with poles and trophies from both banks. Smoke from the Red Banner camp upriver on clear mornings.
Best practiceShow, do not lecture. When players ask “what happened here?”, answer with what they can walk to and touch. Repeat marks across sessions until the table has changed them. Conquest leaves traces; you need not decorate every scene with them.

Craft themes and side threads — then leave engagement to the players. Weave them through the main pressure so the world feels deep. Do not force a path through them.

ExampleThreads you hold ready: Sera’s remnant wants captives returned; the Red Banner wants the ford; Torven wants another campaign before the loot runs out; House Asren wants the captives broken to field work; a clerk still claims to speak for the dead king of Kelarn’s seal. Themes under them: occupation without knowing how to rule, guilt or denial for the sack, legitimacy without a throne, the cost of winning. None of these is a required path. Any can rise if the players lean that way — and each advances if they do not.
Best practiceSide stories are not clutter; they are how a campaign feels lived-in. Prep each thread as a situation: people with goals, pressure that worsens if ignored, more than one way in. Weave themes through the main strain — food, ground, legitimacy, the cost of conquest — without scripting which scenes the table must play. Participation rests with the players. That is not “no side plots”; it is no railroading. If they never take a thread, the world still moves; if they take it, you already know who wants what.

Set starting Fortunes (Vitality, Cohesion, Surplus, Standing, Tradition) as soft 0–3 from the facts above. Store them when the campaign record is created. Early play may correct them.

ExampleVitality 2 (war-hardened, but worn). Cohesion 1 (Torven’s pure war-band and House Asren’s captive-drivers already pull apart). Surplus 1 (loot thin, no real harvest of their own). Standing 1 (neighbours treat the Vardmark as raiders and despoilers, not a realm). Tradition 2 (they still know who they were on the march — not yet what occupation makes of them).
Best practiceFortunes are community weather for every scene — PCs and NPCs. Derive them from material facts, not from a wish to sound epic. If the board looks comfortable after conquest and winter, re-check the strain list.

What the Players Receive

Section titled “What the Players Receive”
  • The ranked list of campaign seeds (examples or your own)
  • The three system constraints stated once

Nothing else.

When seeds are chosen and the community frame exists, proceed to Character Creation.
